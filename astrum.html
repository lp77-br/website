Security Assessment — Astrum (app.astrum.mobile)

Security research on an Android app, published with the developer's explicit permission. Updated November 15, 2026.

TL;DR

Security assessment of a GPS-based territory-conquest Android app. I found 11 findings — 1 high (IDOR in profiles: email of any user readable by UUID; the same RLS gap also exposes the rest of the profile row), 6 medium (mass user enumeration, PATCH on sensitive columns, two GPX-import validation flaws, user enumeration via signup, undisclosed tracking SDKs), 3 low (excessive GPS track exposure — privacy-by-design, Mapbox secret token, ATS hardening) and 1 informational.

Severity ratings reflect operational impact. Wherever the operational rating differs from the CVSS 3.1 base score — or where no CVSS is applicable — the rationale is stated in the finding's severity line.

All flaws were reported to the developer before any public disclosure. On 09/24 the developer stated that some table-related findings had been fixed, without enumerating which; no independent post-fix verification was performed. No real user data was stored, shared, or used for any purpose beyond the technical confirmation of the vulnerabilities.

1. Context

I found the app by chance (a video by the developer himself on TikTok). Astrum is a GPS-based territory-conquest game — the user runs/walks closing loops that become "territories" on the map, with a public ranking per conquered area. At the time of testing the app was already live: the Google Play listing showed roughly 5,000 downloads, and the developer reports up to 20,000 users across Android and iOS.

I started testing through the official interface, without reverse engineering. After finding the first issues, I contacted the developer, who authorized continuing the tests. From there, I deepened the analysis with interception tools and traffic inspection.

The app is under active development by a solo developer, which contextualizes some of the issues found (overloaded functions, absence of legal documentation, etc.). The UI was built with the help of Rork (AI app builder), but all backend logic, database, RPCs, and game mechanics were developed manually. This distinction explains why some protections were correct (storage, rate limit, Pro-plan RLS) while other tables had configuration gaps.

The only third-party data retrieved was 5 UUIDs from the public ranking (finding 4.3), used solely to confirm enumeration. No sensitive third-party data was collected, stored or shared. All test logs and artifacts were deleted after the assessment for operational security.

4. Findings

How to read severities
The badge is the operational severity. The CVSS 3.1 base score (when applicable) reflects the raw technical vector. When operational severity diverges from the CVSS rating, or when CVSS does not apply, the rationale is stated on the finding's severity line. Findings marked "CVSS: N/A" are regulatory, design or hardening items without a direct attack path.

4.1 — IDOR in profiles: email disclosure of any user

Operational severity High (elevated from CVSS Medium — combined PII under LGPD)CVSS 3.16.5 AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:N/A:N

The profiles table had loose Row Level Security, allowing any authenticated user to read another user's profile by UUID — including their email. The proof was performed against a secondary account I own (attacker session, target UUID); the RLS policy is not scoped per-user, so any other UUID would return the same. Running the query without a field filter also returned the rest of the row (name, country, birth_date, sex); only the email-focused reproduction is shown below for minimal disclosure.

Reproduction:

curl -s "https://<project>.supabase.co/rest/v1/profiles?select=email&id=eq.<target_uuid>" \
  -H "apikey: <publishable_key>" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer <access_token>"

Result: [{"email":"<redacted>"}] — the target account in this proof is one I own; no third-party user email was accessed.

Impact: Email is personal data under LGPD. The same RLS gap also exposes name, country, birth_date and sex on the same row; combined, the aggregate PII is enough for re-identification and doxxing when correlated with the public ranking. This is what justifies High.

Status: Reported on 09/21, 01:30. On 09/24 the developer stated some table-related findings had been fixed, without specifying which. No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): restrict profiles SELECT to the owner (auth.uid() = id) and expose only minimal public fields (e.g. display name) through a dedicated view or RPC. Never expose email, birth_date, country or sex through the public REST surface.

4.2 — Excessive GPS track exposure via API (privacy-by-design)

Operational severity Low (design observation — not treated as a vulnerability)CVSS 3.15.9 AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:N/A:N (theoretical)

The activities table returns path.coordinates (full GPS track) to any authenticated user who knows the activity ID. Cross-user activity visibility is an intentional product feature — the developer explicitly declined to change it on 09/24, citing the design: "a de activities que não mechi porque hoje no app ele deixa os usuários verem as atividades dos outros."

Reproduction:

curl -s "https://<project>.supabase.co/rest/v1/activities?select=*&id=eq.<activity_uuid>" \
  -H "apikey: <publishable_key>" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer <access_token>"

Impact: Even though visibility is intentional, the level of detail exposed allows inferring home, work and training routine from a full track. This is best framed as a privacy-by-design concern, not a classic IDOR. The developer confirmed the visibility is intentional and did not treat it as a bug; I record it here because, combined with 4.1, it materially amplifies doxxing risk.

Recommendation: adopt region-based visibility — the owner sees the full track; other users see only the display name and general area unless they are within the track's region. Public API should expose aggregated data (distance, average pace, timestamp) only.

Status: Reported on 09/21. Developer acknowledged on 09/24 and explicitly declined to change, by design. Recorded here as a design recommendation.

4.3 — Mass user enumeration

Operational severity Medium (data already public via ranking caps severity)CVSS 3.16.4 AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:N/A:N

The query profiles?select=id,name, unfiltered, returned the entire user base, with no mandatory pagination or restriction per authenticated user.

Reproduction:

curl -s "https://<project>.supabase.co/rest/v1/profiles?select=id,name&limit=5" \
  -H "apikey: <publishable_key>" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer <access_token>"

Result: 5 real users (name + UUID) — a subset of the public ranking. The data is already public via ranking, which caps severity at Medium; but the API allows the entire base to be enumerated programmatically.

Impact: Complete user base is enumerable. Combined with 4.1, it forms a direct pipeline of enumeration → email harvesting.

Status: Reported on 09/14 (full write-up) and 09/21. On 09/24 the developer stated that some table-related findings had been fixed, without specifying which. No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): mandatory pagination, per-user scoping (friends / region), and no unfiltered select=id,name on profiles.

4.4 — PATCH on sensitive columns of profiles

Operational severity Medium (is_admin consumption not exhaustively verified)CVSS 3.16.5 AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:N/I:H/A:N

The profiles table accepted PATCH on sensitive columns via REST API (is_admin, total_points, total_conquests, total_area, territories_owned). The write was accepted (HTTP 200) even though the UI does not render those columns.

Reproduction:

curl -s -X PATCH "https://<project>.supabase.co/rest/v1/profiles?id=eq.<self_uuid>" \
  -H "apikey: <publishable_key>" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer <access_token>" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -H "Prefer: return=representation" \
  -d '{"is_admin": true, "total_points": 999999}'

Result: 200 — columns modified.

Impact: Stats are computed by RPC and not displayed by the UI, and is_admin is apparently not consumed by any RPC/Edge Function — but this was not exhaustively verified. If any server-side authorization path reads is_admin from profiles, this becomes direct privilege escalation. Kept as Medium pending confirmation.

Status: Reported on 09/21, 06:00. On 09/24 the developer stated that some table-related findings had been fixed, without specifying which. No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): column-level RLS; stats computed server-side only; is_admin never writable from the client, and ideally never stored on the same row as user-writable fields.

4.5 — GPX import accepts future dates

Operational severity Medium (integrity impact on game economy)CVSS 3.16.5 AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:N/I:H/A:N

The GPX import feature accepted files with a future timestamp, without server-side validation. I tested with a one-year lead time and the territory was created, with Stars credited.

Why this matters beyond the obvious: The app resolves territory conflicts by the most recent activity (if I ran yesterday and you ran today, the territory is yours). A GPX with a future date "reserves" the territory indefinitely — no real run can ever overwrite it, effectively denying access to other players. The integrity impact on the game economy justifies Medium on its own.

Status: Reported on 09/14, 21:50. Developer acknowledged the issue on the same day. GPX is trivially reproducible; no logs retained. No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): server-side validation timestamp <= now(), plus a configurable tolerance window (e.g. UTC clock skew ≤ 5 min).

4.6 — GPX import accepts open-ocean coordinates

Operational severity MediumCVSS 3.16.5 AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:N/I:H/A:N

The server does not validate whether the activity occurred on dry land. A GPX centered on an oceanic coordinate (e.g., -23.5000, -42.5000, South Atlantic) was accepted and created a territory.

Impact: No geospatial validation on the backend — territories can be created in restricted, military or inaccessible areas.

Status: Reported on 09/14, 21:50. Developer acknowledged the issue on the same day. GPX is trivially reproducible; no logs retained. No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): minimum viable checks are trivial — bbox per supported region, land-mask lookup (e.g. geopandas / OSM land polygons) and a delta T sanity check.

4.7 — User enumeration via signup screen

Operational severity MediumCVSS 3.15.3 AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N

The signup screen returns a distinct message ("This email is already registered") when the email already exists, allowing enumeration of the user base. Precondition for credential stuffing.

Status: Reported on 09/14 (full write-up). No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): return a generic response ("if this email exists, you will receive a confirmation message") regardless of existence, and rate-limit per IP + per email.

4.8 — Mapbox secret token (sk.) exposed in bundle

Operational severity Low (reduced from CVSS Medium — token has no administrative scopes)CVSS 3.15.3 AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N

The APK's app.config.js exposes a Mapbox secret token (sk.eyJ1Ijoi...) in the RNMAPBOX_MAPS_DOWNLOAD_TOKEN field. The token has no administrative scopes, but allows quota and bandwidth abuse (tile download loop on the developer's account).

Status: Reported on 09/29, 19:21. The developer acknowledged the same day and stated the token would be rotated immediately. No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): rotate the token, use a public pk. or URL-restricted token in the client, and keep sk. strictly server-side.

4.9 — NSAllowsArbitraryLoads: true in iOS infoPlist

Operational severity Low / hardeningCVSS 3.1N/A — configuration hardening, no direct attack path

The app.config.js sets NSAllowsArbitraryLoads: true inside ios.infoPlist, globally disabling App Transport Security (ATS) on iOS. Expands the MITM surface on iOS, even with certificate pinning active at higher layers.

Status: Reported on 09/14 (full write-up). No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): remove the global override; if specific domains require exceptions, use NSExceptionDomains.

4.10 — Undisclosed tracking SDKs

Operational severity Medium (LGPD: High — regulatory exposure)CVSS 3.1N/A — regulatory finding, no technical exploit path

The bundle includes analytics/tracking SDKs from more than 17 vendors — the most prominent are Firebase, Mapbox Telemetry and RevenueCat; the full list is withheld here for security. At the time of testing the app had no privacy policy and no terms of use. On 09/24, informed of the undisclosed SDKs, the developer replied that they did not know about this specific issue and would check which SDKs were actually collecting data and what needed to be declared in the privacy policy. Separately, the developer confirmed having previously been aware that a privacy policy and terms of use were still needed.

Impact: Potential violation of Art. 6 and Art. 9 of the LGPD — purpose, transparency and legal basis for analytics/tracking are not communicated. Operational severity is Medium (not High) because the item is regulatory rather than exploitable; the LGPD exposure itself is High.

Status: Reported on 09/24, 17:29. Developer acknowledged the same day and committed to review. No independent post-fix verification was performed.

Recommendation (best practice): publish a privacy policy and terms of use; enumerate SDKs, data collected, purpose and legal basis; obtain opt-in consent where the legal basis is consent.

4.11 — Observation: missing maximum-distance validation (not tested)

Operational severity InformationalCVSS 3.1N/A — not tested (out of self-imposed safety scope)

I considered importing a GPX with an extremely high distance to check whether the server enforces a limit. I did not execute it because, in the absence of validation (proven in findings 4.5 and 4.6), a track of that magnitude could destabilize older devices or overload the server during geometry processing. I reported it as a technical suspicion without executing it.

5. Good practices observed

  • Supabase storage protected: the activity-imports bucket is correctly isolated per user.
  • Local tampering detection: the app detects modification of the local pro_enabled flag and forces logout. Actual plan validation is server-side.
  • Partial anti-cheat: the system detects obvious anomalies (geographic jumps, impossible paces) and covered a previous incident. It reasonably covers the run flow, but not the GPX-import flow.
  • Rate limit on password reset: the 8-digit code has a 1-minute cooldown between attempts, making brute force infeasible.
  • Mapbox: the exposed sk. token is correctly restricted (no administrative scopes).
  • Manual backend: the developer manually implemented the backend logic, RPCs, and critical protections. The RLS failures found are punctual and configuration-related, not architectural.
  • Timely remediation and test-data cleanup: the developer reported some table-related findings as fixed on 09/24, within ~72 hours of the 09/21 report. The developer also deleted the test territory on 09/14, 23:01, to avoid polluting the public map.

Additional note (not an application vulnerability)

On 09/17, while reviewing the developer's own TikTok video, I noticed a partially exposed backend URL that could have eased discovery of the admin panel. The developer was informed on the same day and acknowledged the warning. This is an operational-security tip, not an application finding, and is recorded here only for completeness.

6. Conclusion

Astrum has a reasonable security baseline in specific areas, but presented configuration flaws in specific tables: misconfigured RLS on tables holding personal data, acceptance of PATCH on authorization columns, absence of server-side validation on GPX import, and LGPD compliance gaps (no privacy policy or terms, 17+ undisclosed tracking SDKs). The most serious finding — email disclosure via the profiles RLS gap (with the same gap exposing the rest of the row) — was reported immediately with reproduction steps and remediation direction.

Timeline: some table-related findings were reported as fixed on 09/24 (three days after the 09/21 report); other items were acknowledged; publication was explicitly authorized by the developer. The app has continued to evolve; no independent post-fix verification was performed at the time of writing.

This report was produced independently, with explicit authorization from the developer, including permission to publish it in my portfolio and disclose the application's name. Publication occurred after the developer reported remediation of the table-related findings on 09/24; no independent post-fix verification was performed. No real user data is included.
